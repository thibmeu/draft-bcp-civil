        Principles for Web Traffic Management by Intermediaries
                     draft-meunier-bcp-civil-latest

Abstract

   This document describes principles for web intermediaries that
   balance security needs with open web accessibility.

1.  Introduction

   Web intermediaries such as content delivery networks, security
   services, and edge platforms increasingly implement traffic
   management systems to protect against abuse.  As these systems become
   more sophisticated, there is growing concern about their impact on
   legitimate users and the open nature of the web.

   This document establishes principles for responsible traffic
   management that preserve web accessibility while maintaining
   necessary protections.

2.  Conventions and Definitions

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT",
   "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and
   "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in
   BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all
   capitals, as shown here.

   The following terms are used throughout this document:

   Active signal:  Client property asserted by the client or its
      operator, verifiable by the intermediary, and not assertable by an
      unrelated party

   Passive signal:  Inferred/heuristic client property

   Graduated response:  Configurable action spectrum

   Intermediary:  As defined in Section 3.7 of [RFC9110], an HTTP
      intermediary is a proxy, gateway, or tunnel that acts on behalf of
      either the client or server.

3.  Principles

   The following principles should be followed by web intermediaries to
   balance\ security requirements with web openness:

   1.  Intermediaries must classify traffic by specific behaviors, not
       by client identity

   2.  Intermediaries must offer response options other than block or
       allow

   3.  Intermediaries must favor open protocols for traffic management
       decisions

   4.  Intermediaries must prefer active signals over passive signals

   5.  Intermediaries must document where their services diverge from
       published standards.

3.1.  Behavior-based classification

   Intermediaries must classify traffic by specific behaviors, not by
   client identity.

   Classifications based on inferred client types (such as "bot" or
   "automated") are unreliable as the distinction between human and
   automated web browsing continues to blur.  Modern web applications
   routinely use automation, and browsers themselves perform automated
   requests for prefetching, security scanning, and other legitimate
   purposes.  Human users can be represented by software, including
   automated clients (Section 2 of [RFC8890]).  Representing a user
   implies neither unconditional access nor an override of site policy.

   Behavior-based classification focuses on observable actions that may
   indicate abuse, such as excessive request rates, unusual access
   patterns, or failure to respect machine-readable signals such as
   caching directives [RFC9111] and, for crawlers, the Robots Exclusion
   Protocol [RFC9309].  This approach enables more precise enforcement
   while avoiding false positives based on client characteristics.

   Policies applied to every client must be general, such as not causing
   adverse impact on the site.  More specific expectations apply
   according to the purpose a client declares, such as [CBCP] for
   crawlers.

3.2.  Graduated enforcement

   Intermediaries must default to allowing authenticated traffic,
   subject to site access policy and behavior-based limits, and offer
   response options other than block or allow.

   Binary blocking decisions create unnecessary friction for legitimate
   users while failing to address the spectrum of potentially unwanted
   behavior.  Graduated responses allow intermediaries to apply
   appropriate restrictions based on confidence levels and threat
   severity.  Intermediaries should narrowly target restrictions to the
   relevant behavior, minimizing effects on unrelated users sharing
   infrastructure, applying the scope and granularity considerations in
   Section 4.1.1 of [RFC7754] and Section 4.1.2 of [RFC7754].

   Effective responses include rate limiting (HTTP 429 status code),
   requests to retry after temporary overload (HTTP 503 with Retry-
   After), content modification per site policy (for instance, a summary
   or lower-quality variant), and verification challenges.  Rate
   limiting implementations SHOULD follow emerging standards such as
   [draft-ietf-httpapi-ratelimit-headers] for consistent client
   communication.  These responses can be applied progressively based on
   behavior patterns rather than defaulting to complete blocking for
   ambiguous cases.

   Challenges and content modification can prevent legitimate clients
   from processing a response.  Where site policy otherwise permits
   access, intermediaries should offer a documented recovery or
   alternative access path that does not depend on human interaction or
   a particular browser's capabilities.  Such a path does not guarantee
   access and remains subject to the availability protections in
   Section 4.

3.3.  Protocol neutrality

   Intermediaries must favor open protocols for traffic management
   decisions and communicate responses using standard HTTP semantics.

   Traffic management decisions should be based on standardized
   mechanisms that any client can implement, rather than proprietary
   systems or curated lists.  This allows legitimate automated clients
   to identify themselves appropriately and follow established
   conventions for access control.

   When responding to blocked or restricted requests, intermediaries
   MUST use appropriate HTTP status codes: 403 Forbidden for policy
   violations and 503 Service Unavailable for temporary overload or
   maintenance, as defined in [RFC9110], and 429 Too Many Requests for
   rate limiting, as defined in [RFC6585].  Response headers SHOULD
   provide actionable information about restrictions and recovery paths.
   For 429 and 503 responses, intermediaries SHOULD include Retry-After
   (Section 10.2.3 of [RFC9110]) when they can estimate when the client
   should retry.

   Open protocols enable website owners to express their preferences
   consistently across different intermediaries, rather than managing
   separate configurations for each vendor's proprietary system.

3.4.  Active signals over passive fingerprints

   Intermediaries must prefer active signals over passive signals.

   Passive fingerprinting techniques identify users without their
   participation, and create false positives for users sharing IP
   addresses, using privacy tools, or connecting from networks with
   unusual characteristics.  These techniques also degrade over time as
   clients change behavior or implement countermeasures.

   Active signals provide assurance about the asserted property, because
   the client or its operator positively asserts a property that the
   intermediary can verify.  Examples include HTTP Message Signatures
   [RFC9421] profiles such as [draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol],
   Privacy Pass tokens [RFC9577], published IP ranges as recommended by
   [CBCP], and forward-confirmed reverse DNS.

   Note that what differs is not necessarily the data itself.  An
   observed IP address is a passive signal, while the same address
   published by its operator is an active signal.

   Authentication and privacy-preserving tokens can improve traffic
   management, but do not by themselves resolve how legitimate clients
   without recognized credentials or supported challenge capabilities
   are treated.  Absence of an active signal does not itself establish
   abuse.

3.5.  Implementation transparency

   Intermediaries must document where their services diverge from
   published standards.

   Intermediaries that develop or promote standards operate multiple
   services that may implement those standards differently.  An
   intermediary that promotes a standard should disclose where its
   services do not follow it.

   While documenting every deviation may not be practical,
   intermediaries should prioritize disclosure in areas where they have
   established published guidance.

   Where safe and feasible, intermediaries should distinguish explicit
   site-policy denial from suspected abuse or temporary overload, and
   provide operators enough information to investigate unintended
   blocking.  This need not disclose detection rules or sensitive
   signals.

4.  Security Considerations

   Authentication does not by itself establish authorization or harmless
   behavior.  Authenticated clients remain subject to site access policy
   and behavior-based limits.  The preference for active signals does
   not require clients to identify themselves to access public
   resources.

   Client assertions can be spoofed.  Intermediaries should verify
   active signals using the relevant mechanism, including the
   authenticity and integrity of published identity data, before using
   them in access decisions.

   Protecting availability can require immediate blocking or dropping
   connections.  Graduated enforcement does not require trying every
   response first (Section 7.2 of [RFC6585]).  Intermediaries should
   also limit the resources spent on signal verification and challenges.

   Active identifiers and passive fingerprints can enable tracking.
   Intermediaries should minimize the collection and retention of
   identifying data and prefer mechanisms that disclose only the
   property needed for a decision.
