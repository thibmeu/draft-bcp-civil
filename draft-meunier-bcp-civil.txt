        Principles for Web Traffic Management by Intermediaries
                     draft-meunier-bcp-civil-latest

Abstract

   This document describes principles for web intermediaries that
   balance security needs with open web accessibility.

1.  Introduction

   Web intermediaries such as content delivery networks, security
   services, and edge platforms increasingly implement traffic
   management systems to protect against abuse.  As these systems become
   more sophisticated, there is growing concern about their impact on
   legitimate users and the open nature of the web.

   This document establishes principles for responsible traffic
   management that preserve web accessibility while maintaining
   necessary protections.

2.  Conventions and Definitions

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT",
   "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and
   "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in
   BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all
   capitals, as shown here.

   The following terms are used throughout this document:

   Active signal:  Client property positively asserted by the client or
      its operator, and verifiable by the intermediary

   Passive signal:  Inferred/heuristic client property

   Graduated response:  Configurable action spectrum

   Intermediary:  As defined in Section 3.7 of [RFC9110], an HTTP
      intermediary is a proxy, gateway, or tunnel that acts on behalf of
      either the client or server.

3.  Principles

   The following principles should be followed by web intermediaries to
   balance\ security requirements with web openness:

   1.  Intermediaries must classify traffic by specific behaviors, not
       by client identity

   2.  Intermediaries must offer response options other than block or
       allow

   3.  Intermediaries must favor open protocols for traffic management
       decisions

   4.  Intermediaries must prefer positively identifiable signals over
       passive fingerprinting

   5.  Intermediaries must document where their services diverge from
       published standards.

3.1.  Behavior-based classification

   Intermediaries must classify traffic by specific behaviors, not by
   client identity.

   Classifications based on inferred client types (such as "bot" or
   "automated") are unreliable as the distinction between human and
   automated web browsing continues to blur.  Modern web applications
   routinely use automation, and browsers themselves perform automated
   requests for prefetching, security scanning, and other legitimate
   purposes.

   Behavior-based classification focuses on observable actions that may
   indicate abuse, such as excessive request rates, unusual access
   patterns, or failure to respect machine-readable signals such as
   caching directives [RFC9111] and the Robots Exclusion Protocol
   [RFC9309].  This approach enables more precise enforcement while
   avoiding false positives based on client characteristics.

   Policies applied to every client must be general, such as not causing
   adverse impact on the origin.  More specific expectations apply
   according to the purpose a client declares, such as [CBCP] for
   crawlers.

3.2.  Graduated enforcement

   Intermediaries must default to allowing authenticated traffic, and
   offer response options other than block or allow.

   Binary blocking decisions create unnecessary friction for legitimate
   users while failing to address the spectrum of potentially unwanted
   behavior.  Graduated responses allow intermediaries to apply
   appropriate restrictions based on confidence levels and threat
   severity.

   Effective responses include rate limiting (HTTP 429 status code),
   request delays (HTTP 503 with Retry-After), content modification, and
   verification challenges.  Rate limiting implementations SHOULD follow
   emerging standards such as [draft-ietf-httpapi-ratelimit-headers] for
   consistent client communication.  These responses can be applied
   progressively based on behavior patterns rather than defaulting to
   complete blocking for ambiguous cases.

3.3.  Protocol neutrality

   Intermediaries must favor open protocols for traffic management
   decisions and communicate responses using standard HTTP semantics.

   Traffic management decisions should be based on standardized
   mechanisms that any client can implement, rather than proprietary
   systems or curated lists.  This allows legitimate automated clients
   to identify themselves appropriately and follow established
   conventions for access control.

   When blocking or restricting access, intermediaries MUST use
   appropriate HTTP status codes as defined in [RFC9110]: 403 Forbidden
   for policy violations, 429 Too Many Requests for rate limiting, and
   503 Service Unavailable for temporary restrictions.  Response headers
   SHOULD provide actionable information about restrictions and recovery
   paths.

   Open protocols enable website owners to express their preferences
   consistently across different intermediaries, rather than managing
   separate configurations for each vendor's proprietary system.

3.4.  Active signals over passive fingerprints

   Intermediaries must prefer positively identifiable signals over
   passive fingerprinting.

   Passive fingerprinting techniques create false positives for users
   sharing IP addresses, using privacy tools, or connecting from
   networks with unusual characteristics.  These techniques also degrade
   over time as clients change behavior or implement countermeasures.

   Active signals provide stronger assurance of client legitimacy,
   because the client or its operator positively asserts a property that
   the intermediary can verify.  Examples include HTTP Message
   Signatures profiles such as
   [draft-meunier-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol], Privacy Pass tokens
   [RFC9577], published IP ranges as required by [CBCP], and forward-
   confirmed reverse DNS.

3.5.  Implementation transparency

   Intermediaries must document where their services diverge from
   published standards.

   Intermediaries that develop or promote standards operate multiple
   services that may implement those standards differently.  An
   intermediary that promotes a standard should disclose where its
   services do not follow it.

   While documenting every deviation may not be practical,
   intermediaries should prioritize disclosure in areas where they have
   established published guidance.  Consistent disclosure demonstrates
   accountability and encourages broader industry adoption.
